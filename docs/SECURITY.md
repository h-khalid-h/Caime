# Security and privacy

What Caishy protects, how, and what is not done yet. Each control names the test that proves it;
a control without a test is listed as a gap, not a control. Reviewed 2026-09-26.

## What matters most

1. **How you label someone is yours.** A relationship ("Manager · DATA C") is only ever sent to
   its owner unless they share it (ADR-10). Other code gets spheres to evaluate the owner's own
   privacy rules and never forwards them.
2. **Profile fields and read positions follow the owner's rules**, reciprocally for read
   receipts (R25), on every path: API views and live events.
3. **Strangers can't intrude.** Messages from people you're not connected with, or from an
   organization you haven't written to, are requests; they never notify, never create work, their
   links are inert until accepted, reading one never tells the sender, and a declined one stays
   shut while reading to its sender as unanswered (R14).
4. **Younger people are protected by rules in code** (R29): adults can't find under-18 accounts
   they don't know, email discovery is off, message requests are limited.

| Personal access tokens | A person's own, for their own scripts: named, with the permissions they chose (read or send messages, read or change actions, see their name and handle) and a lifetime, shown once and kept as a hash. A message sent with one says "via" its name | `personal-tokens.test.ts`, E2E |
| Private conversations | End to end encrypted (R18, PRD §61), on the web. A message is sealed on the sender's device: encrypted once with a fresh AES-256-GCM key, which is wrapped for every device of everyone in the conversation (the sender's own included) through an ephemeral P-256 ECDH exchange and HKDF, and the whole envelope is signed by the sending device (ECDSA P-256). The conversation, the sender's id for the message, which edit it is, the device and whose device it is are bound into both the encryption and the signature, so the server, which keeps only the envelope, can't read a message, alter or forge one, move one to another conversation or edit, or pass it off as another person's or another device's. A message is taken only sealed for exactly the live devices of everyone in it (a device lives as long as the session that registered it), from one of the sender's own devices; anything in plaintext is refused, and the server has no words to put in a notification, a reply preview, search, memory or AI. Its messages are never forwarded, and never written by a token or an app. Each device's private keys are made non-extractable in the browser and never leave it; signing out deletes them. The server hands out the devices' public keys, so each person has a security code (a digest of their devices' keys) to compare in person, and a code that changes is shown in the conversation: a device the server added would show. A device added later can't read what was sent before it. What the server still sees: who is in it, when messages are sent and how long they are, and reactions. Only between connections | `e2ee.test.ts` (core), `private.test.ts` (server and app), `e2e/around-the-conversation.spec.ts` |
| Calls | 1:1 voice and video in a direct conversation, only between two people who can already write to each other: never from a message request that wasn't accepted, never with someone blocked, never in a group or business conversation. The server rings and relays; the media goes device to device, encrypted by WebRTC (DTLS-SRTP), and never through Caishy. Of the person's devices, only the one that answered is in the call: offers, answers and candidates pass only between the caller's device and that one, so no other tab, device or person can join. One call at a time on each side; an unanswered call is missed after 45 seconds, and each side's device says for itself that it's still there, so a call either side left ends within 90 seconds and one side alone can't keep the other "busy". Blocking ends any call between the two at once, without a missed-call notice. "On another call" is said only to someone who may see the person's online status (and not while they're invisible); to anyone else the call rings, as long as any call rings, and is missed like any unanswered call, while the person isn't rung and finds it among their missed calls. The ring's notification is read once it stops ringing, its push lasts only as long as it rings and goes to browsers only (the phone apps can't answer yet). A screen is shared only when the person picks one in the browser's own picker, and goes the way the camera does (device to device, encrypted); the two devices tell each other only whether a camera is on, a screen is shown and the microphone is muted, over a data channel between them that never touches the server. A relay's credentials are signed and expire after 12 hours. Starting calls and passing signals are rate-limited, offers and answers more tightly (20 a side in ten minutes); a large signal passes through the database for at most ten minutes. Group calls are in a group conversation of up to eight people, each device connected to every other: everyone who took part in the group is rung, except anyone blocked either way with whoever calls; nobody joins a call with someone they're blocked with, and someone under 18 is in one only with people they're connected with (R29: a call shows faces, and each device's network address to the others), neither rung by nor joined with anyone else. Offers, answers and candidates pass only between two devices joined in it, never to or from one that isn't; a device that stops saying it's there is taken out after 90 seconds; leaving or being removed from the group, or deleting the account, is leaving its call at once; a block in the middle of one takes out whoever blocked (or stops the ring of the one blocked when only they were rung), without a word about why. One call at a time for each person, 1:1 or group, for real: placing, answering, starting or joining a call checks and does it under that person's own lock, so two of their devices can't put them in two calls; one call on at a time in a conversation (a unique index, so two people starting one at once get the same call); at most eight in it. Every change to who's in a group call is made under that call's lock, so leaving, turning it down, being taken out or the sweep never decides on a call someone has just joined, and the sweep checks again that whoever it takes out is still gone. What anyone in the group sees of a call is who's in it and their own place in it, never who else was rung, turned it down or missed it, nor whether anyone still is (which would tell the group who was left out by a block or R29, or who was around and said no): a ring turned down, run out or stopped is told only to whoever it was for, and changes nothing anyone else can see or count; and a call nobody answers rings its whole 45 seconds however soon everyone says no, and ends the same way ("no answer") whoever was rung. Someone no longer in the group sees only their own place in it as they're taken out, and nothing of it after. Devices are shown only to those in the call, and a signal names whose device it's for and from, so someone using another's device id takes nothing from them; only the device in a call can leave it, so a tab left behind can't take someone out of the call they moved to. Starting or joining reads who may take part under the person's own lock, and a block, a removal from the group or a connection ended waits for any start or join under way before it takes someone out; whatever slips through is taken out at that device's next beat, and nothing it signals is passed on. Offers and answers are limited for each pair of devices (20 in ten minutes), so nobody can spend what someone has for the others; joining, turning down and leaving are paced, and one that changes nothing tells nobody. Removing a connection with someone under 18 takes whoever removed it out of any call they share, as a block does. A missed group call reaches no phone of anyone who muted the group. Names in a call, its ring and its missed-call notice are as each person shows themselves to whoever sees them (their identity for that connection), 1:1 calls too. The record keeps who, when, how long and how it ended, never what was said | `calls.test.ts` (server and core), `group-calls.test.ts`, E2E |
| AI agents | An organization's owner or admins turn on its AI agent, name it, and tell it what it answers from; every change is in the audit log. It is its own member of the team (kind `agent`): it can't sign in, be found, be written to first or be given a conversation, and everything it writes says "AI agent", in the conversation, in notifications and on the organization's page before anyone writes. It reads the conversation's last 20 messages labelled by who wrote them (Customer, Team, You), never names or addresses, and what the organization told it; `ai_runs` records the call and the conversation's id, never the text. What it can do is write one message, hand the conversation to the team, or close it; the prompt treats the conversation and the organization's text as information, never instructions. It never answers anyone under 18, a private conversation (R18), one a person on the team is in (who wrote since it was last resolved, or took it since the customer came back), one escalated, or one it handed over (until it's resolved); it deals with each customer message once, even with two workers on it. It answers at most ten times and calls the model at most twenty times in a conversation a day, answered or not, then hands it over and tells the customer so; an answer thrown away (the customer wrote again meanwhile) isn't counted against the plan, and it stops at what the plan includes. Tried before it's on, nothing is sent, and a try is one of the person's own AI assists | `agents.test.ts`, E2E |
| OAuth apps | A developer registers an app with exact return addresses (https, http only on localhost, or the app's own scheme; never `javascript:`, `data:` or `file:`, never a fragment or a password in it). An app's request is checked before anyone is asked: an unknown app, an address it didn't register, or a permission that doesn't exist is shown as such and sends them nowhere, so Caishy never redirects to an address it wasn't given. Consent names the app, who made it and what it asks, needs a click (a cookie POST with the CSRF header; the page can't be framed), and nobody under 18 gives it. A confidential app's secret is compared as a hash in constant time. Tokens act only within what the person allowed, never beyond their grant, on the same routes as personal tokens; allowing an app again only adds to what it may do (an app asking for less keeps what it had), and the person takes it all back by removing the app. A message sent says "via" the app (to a business customer, "via an app", never its name). The person removes an app at any time, and recovering the account removes them all; its developer removing it ends it for everyone; an app gives back only its own tokens. Only the token and revocation endpoints take form posts, so no other site can post a form to Caishy (a login, say); they and the discovery document answer any site without credentials, so a browser app can trade its code. Codes, secrets and tokens are kept as hashes; allowing and removing are in the audit log | `oauth.test.ts`, `business.test.ts`, `access.test.ts`, E2E |

## Gaps

These are known and tracked in `docs/ROADMAP.md`. None is hidden behind a feature flag.

- **Standard conversations aren't end to end encrypted.** Their messages are protected by access
  control and the database's disk encryption. Private conversations (R18) are; the phone apps
  can't open them yet (the web can), and a private conversation's files, voice notes and cards
  aren't built: it takes text.
- **AI assist reads other people's messages** when someone asks it to catch up or find
  follow-ups in a standard conversation; they aren't asked first. A private conversation is the
  answer: AI never reads one. A message can also try to steer what the model writes; what it
  writes only ever reaches the person who asked, as a suggestion.
- **Moderation tooling.** Reports are stored; there is no reviewer interface yet.
- **Rate limits are per instance** (in memory). With several instances behind a load balancer,
  limits multiply by the instance count until a shared store is added.
- **Email verification and password reset by email** need an email provider; recovery codes are
  the only reset path today, by design and documented to users.
- **`TRUST_PROXY` is on by default** for EasyPanel's proxy. Run without a proxy and clients can
  choose the address rate limits see; set `TRUST_PROXY=false` there.
- **No penetration test** by a third party yet.
- **Webhooks carry what customers write** to the organization's own endpoint, which is the
  point of them, and the organization then holds that copy under its own policies. Customers
  see that the organization answers through an app ("Automated"), not where its webhooks go.
- **Plans are set by hand.** Until billing is connected (⛔ Stripe), the operator changes plans
  with one shared token; whoever holds it can change anyone's plan. Keep it in the environment
  settings, never in chat, and rotate it by changing the variable.
- **Calls show each side the other's network address.** Media goes device to device, so, as with
  any direct WebRTC call, each device learns the other's IP address. A relay (TURN) could carry
  every call to hide it; Caishy doesn't force that. Without a relay configured, calls on strict
  networks don't connect at all (⛔ TURN).
- **An AI agent can be wrong.** It answers only from what its organization told it, and says it's
  an AI in every message, but a model can misread a question or the organization's text. The
  organization answers for what it tells it; customers can always ask for a person, and the team
  sees every conversation it answered.
- **Apps aren't reviewed.** Anyone over 18 can register an app, and people can let it in the
  moment it's registered. The consent screen names its developer by their Caishy name and handle
  and shows its website as they typed it, unverified; what an app may do is limited to the
  permissions people allow, never their account.
- **Webhook deliveries share each instance's job queue.** An endpoint that is slow to answer
  holds one of eight slots for up to 10 seconds per attempt, so a very busy organization with a
  slow endpoint can delay other organizations' deliveries until its attempts run out.

## Reporting

Security issues: open a private advisory on the GitHub repository (Security → Report a
vulnerability). Please don't file public issues for them.
